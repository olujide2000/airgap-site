Transcribing without uploading

Most transcription tools work by sending your file to a server. If that's not an option for your work, here's what the alternative actually involves — the honest version, including the trade-offs.

What "uploading" really means

When you drop a recording into a cloud transcription service, several things happen that are easy to overlook:

  • A copy of your recording now exists on infrastructure you don't control.
  • It may be retained after processing — sometimes for months, per the terms of service.
  • It may be accessible to that company's staff, contractors, or sub-processors.
  • It can be compelled by legal process served on them, which you may never be told about.
  • It becomes part of their breach surface as well as yours.

For a marketing podcast, none of that matters. For a privileged client conversation, a patient session, or an interview with a source who asked for protection, every line of it matters.

How local transcription works instead

The speech recognition models that power modern transcription — OpenAI's Whisper family chief among them — are open, and they run on ordinary computers. You don't need a data centre. You need the model file on your hard drive and software to feed audio through it.

The recording is read from your disk, processed by your own processor, and written back out as text. No network connection is involved at any point. You can verify this the blunt way: disconnect from the internet and watch it work anyway.

The honest trade-off: it's slower

This is the part most pages like this leave out. Cloud services run on hardware far beyond anything in a laptop. Local transcription is slower, and you should know by how much before you commit.

Measured on a mid-range 2021 laptop with no dedicated AI hardware, transcribing a real one-hour recording:

ModeOne hour of audioTrade-off
Fastabout 9 minutesVery good on clear audio; struggles more with heavy accents and crosstalk
Accurateabout 24 minutesNoticeably better on difficult audio; worth it for final transcripts

Two things soften that. First, there's no upload time — a one-hour recording is often several hundred megabytes, and on a typical connection you'd spend a meaningful chunk of that 9 minutes just sending it. Second, it's unattended: you queue files and walk away, or leave a batch running overnight.

The real comparison isn't "9 minutes versus instant." It's "9 minutes on my own machine versus upload time, queue time, processing, and a permanent copy of a confidential recording on someone else's server."

What you need

  • A reasonably modern computer. Any machine from roughly 2018 onward handles this. A dedicated graphics card helps but isn't required.
  • Disk space for the model — a few hundred megabytes to about 1.6GB depending on the accuracy level. Downloaded once, then yours.
  • Software that doesn't make you build it yourself. The models are free; the awkward part is the tooling around them.

That last point is the real barrier. The underlying technology is free and open. Making it something a lawyer or clinician can install and use without touching a command line is the actual work — see the Windows options for an honest comparison of what exists.
