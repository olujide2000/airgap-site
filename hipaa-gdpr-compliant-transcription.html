Transcription, HIPAA and GDPR

Local processing genuinely changes your compliance position — but not in the way most marketing pages imply. Here's an honest account of what it does and doesn't do.

This is not legal advice. I'm a software developer, not a lawyer or a data protection officer. Use this to have a better-informed conversation with yours.

What changes when processing is local

Both HIPAA and GDPR are largely concerned with what happens when data moves to someone else. Send a recording to a cloud transcription service and you've engaged a third party to process protected data on your behalf. That brings obligations:

  • Under HIPAA, that vendor is typically a Business Associate, and you generally need a signed Business Associate Agreement before sending them any protected health information. Many consumer transcription services will not sign one, or only on their most expensive tier.
  • Under GDPR, that vendor is a processor. You need a lawful basis, a data processing agreement under Article 28, a record of the processing, and — if they're outside your jurisdiction — a valid transfer mechanism.
  • Under research ethics rules, your approval may simply not permit third-party processing at all, regardless of what agreements exist.

When the transcription happens entirely on a machine you already control, using software that makes no network connection, no third party is processing anything. The data stays inside a boundary you've already accounted for. That doesn't make the obligations disappear — it removes an entire category of them from the picture.

The honest framing: local processing doesn't make you compliant. It removes one of the harder things to be compliant about. Your recordings are still protected data, and everything you already do to safeguard them still applies.

What doesn't change

This is the part worth being clear about, because a tool that oversells here is a tool that gets you in trouble:

  • The recording is still protected data. Sitting on your laptop, it carries the same obligations it always did.
  • Your device security is still your responsibility — disk encryption, access control, backups, and what happens if the laptop is lost or stolen.
  • Retention and deletion rules still apply. Local storage makes it easier to forget you still hold something you should have destroyed.
  • Consent and lawful basis are unaffected. Whatever you needed to record the conversation, you still need.
  • Your own organisation's policy may still require review of any new software touching sensitive data, local or not.

Questions worth asking of any transcription tool

Including this one. If a vendor can't answer these plainly, that's informative:

  • Does the audio leave the device at any point — including for licence checks, crash reports, or analytics?
  • Does it work with the network disconnected? Can I verify that myself?
  • Is an account or sign-in required to use it?
  • Where are transcripts and temporary files written, and are they cleaned up?
  • If a cloud service: will you sign a BAA or an Article 28 DPA, at my price tier?

Where Airgap sits

Airgap is being built so the answers are: no, the audio never leaves the device; yes, it works disconnected; no account required; and no licence check that phones home, because a privacy tool that calls a server to validate itself isn't really one.

What it won't claim is that installing it makes you HIPAA or GDPR compliant. No software does that. It removes the third-party processor from your transcription workflow, which is a real and useful thing, and leaves the rest of your obligations exactly where they were.
